# Currency Volatility Is an Operating Risk: A Practical Framework for Businesses Exposed to Iran

For companies that buy, sell, source, price, or invest across borders, exchange-rate volatility is not merely a financial-market story. It can alter margins, working-capital needs, supplier behavior, customer demand, and the viability of contracts already signed.

Iran is an especially useful case study because changes in the local foreign-exchange market can transmit quickly into business decisions. A manager does not need to trade currencies to have currency exposure. Importers, manufacturers using imported inputs, exporters, retailers, logistics companies, technology firms paying for foreign services, and businesses carrying foreign-currency liabilities can all be affected.

The practical question is therefore not simply, “Where will the exchange rate go?” A more useful question is: “What happens to our business if the exchange rate moves before we can reprice, collect cash, or replace inventory?”

## The four channels of currency exposure

A simple way to analyze currency risk is to separate exposure into four channels.

### 1. Input-cost exposure

If a company relies directly or indirectly on imported materials, machinery, software, components, packaging, or services, a weaker local currency can increase replacement costs. The important figure is often not the accounting cost of inventory already on the shelf, but the cost of replacing it after it is sold.

That distinction matters. A company can appear profitable while selling old inventory and then discover that its cash is insufficient to replenish the same quantity of stock.

### 2. Pricing exposure

Businesses rarely reprice every product instantly. Contracts, customer resistance, regulation, competition, and internal approval processes create delays. Currency volatility can therefore open a gap between rising costs and selling prices.

Managers should measure this “repricing lag” explicitly. A business that needs 30 days to adjust prices faces a different risk from one that can update prices daily.

### 3. Working-capital exposure

When replacement costs rise, companies need more cash to finance the same physical volume of business. Receivables also become more dangerous when inflation and currency depreciation are high because money collected later may buy fewer inputs.

This makes payment terms a currency-risk variable. Shortening receivable periods, renegotiating supplier terms, and reviewing customer credit can sometimes protect a business more effectively than attempting to forecast the exchange rate itself.

### 4. Demand exposure

Currency depreciation can reduce customers’ purchasing power and change the relative attractiveness of imported and domestic goods. Some companies may gain pricing power or export competitiveness, while others face weaker demand as consumers postpone purchases.

The same currency move can therefore create winners and losers across industries.

## Replace forecasts with scenarios

Managers are often tempted to build a budget around one exchange-rate forecast. That creates false precision.

A more resilient approach is to model at least three scenarios: a base case, a moderate adverse move, and a severe adverse move. For each scenario, calculate the effect on gross margin, replacement cost, working-capital requirements, cash runway, receivables, and major contracts.

The objective is not to predict the exact future rate. It is to discover where the business breaks.

For example, if a 10% currency move reduces gross margin only slightly, the company may have adequate resilience. If the same move turns a profitable product line into a loss because prices cannot be adjusted for six weeks, management has identified a concrete vulnerability.

## Build decision triggers

Scenario analysis becomes more useful when it is connected to predetermined actions.

A company might decide that if its relevant exchange-rate benchmark moves beyond a specified threshold, it will shorten price-validity periods, review purchase orders, reduce customer credit, accelerate collections, renegotiate contracts, or change inventory targets.

These triggers reduce the temptation to improvise during volatile markets.

## Watch the rate your business actually faces

A common analytical mistake is to focus on a headline exchange rate that does not correspond to the rate embedded in the company’s transactions.

Managers should identify which rate actually affects replacement costs, supplier quotations, customer pricing, and settlement. They should also track the spread between observable market benchmarks and the effective rate appearing in real transactions.

The purpose of monitoring is not to turn the finance team into currency traders. It is to connect market movement to operating decisions.

## A five-question weekly currency review

A practical management meeting can begin with five questions:

1. Which costs would change first if the currency moved materially this week?
2. How long would it take us to reprice our products or services?
3. How much additional working capital would we need to replace current inventory?
4. Which customers, suppliers, or contracts create the largest mismatch between payment timing and currency exposure?
5. What action will we take if our predefined currency threshold is crossed?

These questions convert exchange-rate volatility from an abstract macroeconomic issue into a manageable operating-risk process.

## The management lesson

Businesses cannot control currency markets, and consistently predicting short-term exchange rates is extraordinarily difficult. They can, however, control how quickly information enters pricing decisions, how much credit they extend, how they structure contracts, how much inventory they carry, and what actions are triggered by large market moves.

That is the core distinction between forecasting currency volatility and managing it.

For businesses exposed to Iran, the exchange rate should be treated as an operating variable alongside sales, inventory, cash flow, and margins. The strongest organizations are not necessarily those that predict the next currency move correctly. They are the ones that know in advance what they will do when it happens.
